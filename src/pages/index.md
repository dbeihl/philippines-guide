This is David's dumb guide to travel in the Philippines. It's broken up into sections, with travel tips along the way. The tips are personal learnings from my trip to Davao and the layover in Manila.

> **Travel tip 0: Drink bottled water only.**
> Higher-end restaurants will have filtered water, but you have to ask. Never assume. Also be cautious of vegetables or fruit that were washed with local water.

## Luggage and what to bring

### Luggage

In terms of luggage, I'd recommend bringing:

1. **A normal roller bag**, especially if you're staying in the cities and using Grab or a taxi.
2. **A sling bag** for walking around: power bank, cash, passport, medicines, etc.
3. **A small backpack for day trips**, especially if you have a partner. You'll want water bottles, sunscreen, OFF! spray or lotion, and snacks, and you'll more than likely buy something. The convenience of the small backpack can't be overstated.

The big-backpack-instead-of-a-suitcase idea is good, and it's best if you're going to be extremely mobile and not using taxis much. I used taxis and rode in my girlfriend's family's cars, so giving up the space wasn't worth it.

> **Travel tip 1: Know where your checked bag goes.**
> If you try to bring your roller bag on the plane, it's very likely they'll ask to check it for free. Here's what that means on the way in: when you land in Manila, you have to pick the bag up at baggage claim, take it through customs, then drop it at the baggage drop counter for your next flight. I didn't know this, and I'm glad I talked to someone in immigration while my bag was sitting on the carousel.
>
> On the way home I did it differently, and it was very efficient. At check-in, ask the agent to send your bag through to your final destination, then confirm you won't need to go to baggage claim at the layover. They'll put a sticker on the back of your boarding pass. Show it to someone at the layover to confirm you don't need to get your bag.
>
> Either way, I wouldn't try to carry a lot onto the plane. Check your bags through to your end destination and bring a backpack on board.

### What to bring

#### For the plane

- Compression socks
- AirPods with noise cancellation
- Wired headphones, because your AirPods will die
- A 3.5 mm to airplane headphone jack adapter, plus a USB-C or Lightning to 3.5 mm adapter
- iPad or laptop. Bring one; you won't need both.
- A slim water bottle
- Sweatshirt and sweatpants for the flights

#### Clothes

- 5 shirts (2 polos, 3 t-shirts)
- 2-3 pairs of shorts
- 7-8 pairs of underwear
- Swim shorts
- Pants, only if you have a formal event. You most likely won't.
- A wash bag for laundry. Buy detergent powder at 7-Eleven for less than $2 and wash clothes in the shower, or have a badass girlfriend who does it for you 😉

#### Money

- $500 cash for each week you'll be there. Convert it to PHP when you can.
- In malls you can use a credit card (Visa or Mastercard).

#### Health

- Sunscreen. An absolute need.
- An extra pair of glasses
- Your medications and supplements, with printed copies of the prescriptions just in case
- Imodium (or buy it there)
- Melatonin for the flights and for sleeping once you get there. I started taking it 8 days before the trip so my body was used to it. Ask your doctor what timing and dose make sense for you.

> **Travel tip 2: Only bring what you can't buy there.**
> The number one tip: only bring things you can't buy in the Philippines. If you have a specific brand of something you love, bring it. But remember, you can get everything there, and cheaply. Don't do what I did. Don't get carried away with videos telling you what you need, then buy stuff you don't need.

## Before you go

### Apps

Download these:

- **eGovPH.** This is where the Philippines eTravel arrival form lives (you can also fill it out at [etravel.gov.ph](https://etravel.gov.ph/)). It only opens within 3 days (72 hours) before you arrive. It's free, so any site that asks you to pay is a scam. Save a picture of the QR code at the end of the process.
- **Grab** for rides and delivery.
- **GCash** for payments and sending money.
- **Klook** to buy an eSIM. Make sure it runs on Globe's network.

Once the eSIM is active:

1. Switch your cellular data to the new eSIM.
2. Turn off your home line.
3. For good measure, turn off data roaming on your home line too.

> **Travel tip 4: Put the eSIM QR code on a second screen.**
> Download the QR code from Klook (or whichever eSIM provider you use) to your iPad, so you can scan it with your phone, since your phone is where the eSIM will live. Or put it on your partner's phone.

> **Travel tip 5: Don't trust public WiFi.**
> Honest take: I didn't trust public WiFi the entire trip, except at my girlfriend's place. Hotels, malls, restaurants: I kept my hotspot on and used mobile data instead. If you do connect to public WiFi, use a VPN. I used one religiously, and I'd recommend you do the same. You can pick one up cheaply before you go.

### Data is cheap, so use it

One of the great things about the Philippines is that mobile data is affordable. Your eSIM plan will likely give you plenty of data for maps, messaging, and Grab rides. Just top it up if you're running low. With a Klook eSIM, you add data in the Klook app. If you pick up a local Globe prepaid SIM, you can reload it through GCash or at 7-Eleven.

### Expect spotty coverage

Even with Globe, coverage can be spotty outside the cities and on more remote islands. Download offline maps (Google Maps lets you do this) before heading somewhere less urban.

## Getting around

If you're staying at a hotel, you can ask the doorman or concierge for a taxi, and they'll get one for you. You can also get a taxi at a mall or the airport. At the airport, don't take the ones right outside, because they charge extra.

Jeepneys and trikes are everywhere. Just wave at them. Make sure you have small bills; you don't want to make it hard on them.

## On the plane

Your goals are simple: eat when they want you to eat, and sleep when you can. I'd focus on trying to sleep. For me, that meant bringing noise-canceling headphones and something to black out the light (see the optional items list). I also used melatonin to fall asleep. I slept about 9 hours on each flight this way and listened to podcasts the rest of the time.

Download these before you board:

- Podcasts
- YouTube videos
- Movies in your favorite app
- A few games (solitaire, sudoku, etc.)

## Let's talk about pooping

### Bidet

Most bathrooms have a bidet: a small handheld sprayer on a hose next to the toilet. Lean forward, spray yourself off, and wipe dry with toilet paper. Test the water pressure first.

**You do not put toilet paper in the toilet.** There's usually a wastebasket for it. It shouldn't be as gross as you think; if it is, you didn't spray enough. Some newer places in the cities are fine with flushing paper, but most places don't have the sewer buildout for it.

### Tabo

In some places you'll have a tabo instead: a small plastic dipper, usually sitting in a bucket of water. This one takes a little more practice for a dumb Westerner. Lean over and pour the water on your butt. Again, you do not put toilet paper in the toilet.

I'd recommend buying a single roll of toilet paper when you get there, or packing one if you have space. Because of the no-flushing rule, I'd also get wet wipes for a cleaner experience overall.

## Etiquette

### Shoes off

In most homes you take your shoes off at the door. You'll usually see a pile of shoes outside; that's your cue. Don't overthink it, just follow what everyone else does. It's respectful to do this in hotels too. You'll typically see slippers.

### Filipinos are incredibly hospitable

Be ready for this. If you visit someone's home, they will feed you. Refusing food can come across as rude, so even if you're not hungry, take a little and be gracious about it. You'll also likely be introduced to what feels like the entire extended family. Just roll with it and enjoy it.

### Blessing elders (mano po)

"Mano po" is a common way to show respect and be polite. You take an elder's hand, bow, and press the back of their hand to your forehead. What shocked me is that younger kids will do this to you. You give them your hand and receive their respect.

### "Po" and "opo"

Filipinos use "po" and "opo" as terms of respect when speaking to elders or strangers ("opo" is a respectful "yes"). You won't be expected to use them, but knowing what they mean helps. If an elder says something to you and you hear "po," they're being polite and respectful toward you.
